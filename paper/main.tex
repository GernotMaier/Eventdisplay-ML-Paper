\begin{abstract}
Ground-based imaging atmospheric Cherenkov telescopes (IACTs) detect the faint
Cherenkov light
emitted by air showers initiated by high-energy gamma rays.
IACTs are the primary instruments to detect these photons above tens of GeV.
Multiple analysis methods exist to reconstruct direction and energy,
with performance and robustness varying between depending on observation conditions
and gamma-ray energy.
This paper presents a machine-learning approach based on gradient-boosted regression trees
that learns from the errors of existing methods and combine their results.
Evaluation based on simulated events and observations of the Crab Nebula shows
significant improvements in angular and energy resolution over existing methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Gamma-ray astronomy uses Imaging Atmospheric Cherenkov Telescopes (IACTs)
to measure the energy and direction of photons from
tens of GeV to hundreds of TeV (e.g., \citep{2009ARA&A..47..523H}).
The telescopes record Cherenkov light from air showers using
large reflectors, pixelated cameras combined with fast electronics and trigger systems.
Modern observatories measure projections of the longitudinal development of air showers
with multiple telescopes simultaneously,
enabling stereoscopic reconstruction of primary direction and energy.
The accuracy of this technique is limited by intrinsic air-shower fluctuations and
telescope photon-detection efficiency to estimated
1 (0.3) arcmin angular resolution at 100 GeV (1 TeV)
\citep{hofmann2006performancelimitscherenkovinstruments}.
Systematic uncertainties from varying observation conditions (e.g., night-sky background light,
atmospheric conditions) further limit the achievable performance and require robust
reconstruction methods.

The fields knows a variety of reconstruction methods for direction and energy estimation,
including robust and efficient geometric methods based on image parameters
\citep{1985ICRC....3..445H, 1997APh.....8....1D, 1999APh....12..135H},
template-model based methods \citep{2001A&A...374..895P,2006APh....25..195L, 2009APh....32..231D,2014APh....56...26P},
and machine-learning-based methods using
Hillas parameters \citep{2024A&A...691A.328A} or full camera images.
Performance varies across methods, with template-model based algorithms achieving
the highest precision at the cost of computational complexity and robustness.

This paper shows that a simple machine-learning-based regression layer
using the XGBoost library \citep{Chen:2016:XST:2939672.2939785}
can improve the performance of existing image-parameter-based
reconstruction methods.
The approach adds a thin code layer to an established analysis pipeline, yielding performance gains
while retaining their robustness.
The explicit goal is to use this method for archival data spanning almost two
decades of VERITAS \citep{2006APh....25..391H} observations
without requiring a complete re-write of the analysis code or low-level reprocessing
of raw data.
The method is sufficiently robust to be easily adapted to different IACT arrays,
as demonstrated for both VERITAS and simulations of the Northern array of the Cherenkov Telescope Array Observatory (CTAO)
\citep{2019scta.book.....C}.

The code developed for this paper is available from \citep{maier_2026_18412988}.
All methods and results presented here are based on the open-source Eventdisplay reconstruction
code \citep{maier_2025_17447664,maier_2026_18412887}.

The paper is structured as follows:
Section 2 describes the data pipeline and feature engineering.
Section 3 discusses the regression targets and performance metrics.
Section 4 presents the model architecture and training strategy.
Section 5 evaluates the performance and Section 6 discusses systematic effects and robustness.
Section 7 concludes with summary and outlook.


\section{Regression Targets and Features}

At the core of this approach is a multi-target regression approach with the following three targets:
logarithm of the primary energy ($\log_{10}(E)$) and the two-dimensional direction offsets
in camera coordinates ($x, y$).
Training features are derived from the Eventdisplay reconstruction pipeline and are
categorized into telescope-level features, stereo-reconstruction features, and features
describing the array layout and observation conditions.
Detailed feature descriptions are provided in Appendix \ref{sec:features}.
Clipping and transformations (e.g., logarithmic scaling of size and energy) are applied
to handle the wide dynamic range of the data and to mitigate the influence of outliers.

Telescope-level features include image (Hillas) parameters such as width, length, size,
and distance for each telescope, along with additional features engineered to correct for
systematic effects like image truncation.
In addition to image size, the brightness of the measurement is described by the
number of pixels after image cleaning and the number of active low-gain pixels (for telescopes
with dual-gain readout such as VERITAS or the CTAO Large-Sized Telescopes).
The relationship between telescope-level features and actual telescopes is
broken by sorting features by image size (largest first) and filling telescopes without
data with not-a-number (NaN) values.
For CTAO, which consist of different telescope types, features are sorted first by
telescope mirror area (largest first) as a proxy for telescope type,
with features for each telescope then sorted by image size (largest first).
The sorting allows to apply the same model to events with different telescope multiplicities.

Stereo reconstruction features include the results of two
different energy and direction reconstruction methods plus intermediate features such
as shower core distance, Cherenkov emission height, and weighting factors
used by during the reconstruction.
For energy reconstruction, the methods include multi-dimensional look-up
tables (table axes are image size, core distance, offset of the observed pointing direction
from the camera centre, zenith and azimuth angle, level of nigh-sky background light) and
boosted-regression-tree (BDT)\footnote{
  BDT-based methods are included in the
  Eventdisplay for both energy and direction reconstruction.
  The implementation is based on the TMVA package \citep{2007physics...3039H}.}
based energy estimators based on a subset of the image parameters.
For direction reconstruction, the standard methods include the intersection of lines method
based on image orientations and a BDT regression-tree based method based on the estimation of
the displacement (disp) of the shower direction from the image centroid.
The disp-BDT method provides both an average energy and direction estimation, plus per-telescope estimates
including estimated errors for each telescope, which are used as features for the learner.

Global observation conditions are described by features such as the number of active telescopes,
the array footprint (the area covered by active telescopes), and the angle of the telescope pointing
with respect to the geomagnetic field.

\begin{itemize}
  \item regression targets: energy, direction (x,y)
  \item multi-target regression
  \item gradient-boosted decision trees (XGBoost)
  \item Gradient-boosted decision trees
  \item Loss functions
  \item Regularization and depth control
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameters:} Optimization of tree depth (max\_depth=10), learning rate (0.1), and subsampling (0.7).
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} \texttt{reg:squarederror} optimized for residuals in spatial and energy domains.
    \item \textbf{Weighted Learning:} Implementation of inverse-count energy weighting and multiplicity weighting (proportional to $N_{images}^2$) to balance the training set.
\end{itemize}
